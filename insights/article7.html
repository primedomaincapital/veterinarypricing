Dental Disease Is the Bill Nobody Budgets For

The estimate arrived on a single sheet of paper at the end of a routine checkup. The dog was ten, healthy enough on the surface — and the vet had found what vets almost always find in a ten-year-old dog. Dental disease.

5 min read · Prime Domain Capital

The estimate arrived on a single sheet of paper, handed over at the end of a routine checkup. The dog was ten, a Labrador, healthy enough on the surface — and the vet had found what vets almost always find in a ten-year-old dog. Dental disease. The estimate listed the cleaning, the anesthesia, the bloodwork, and then the line that changed the tone of the conversation: extractions, priced per tooth, with a range that ran from a few hundred dollars to $2,500 for a complex extraction. The total, if the x-rays showed what the vet expected them to show, would be somewhere between $1,500 and $3,000. The owner had budgeted for the checkup. She had not budgeted for the teeth.

The disease almost every pet has

Dental disease is the most common clinical condition in companion animals, and it is the one owners notice last. The American Veterinary Medical Association reports that more than 80 percent of dogs and 70 percent of cats show signs of periodontal disease by age three. The numbers are not close calls. They are near-universal. A three-year-old dog has a four-in-five chance of already having gum disease, and the owner will not know, because dogs do not complain about their teeth. They stop chewing on one side. They eat more slowly. They drop food. The signs are easy to miss and easy to explain away, and by the time the disease is visible — the red gum line, the loose tooth, the smell — it is advanced.

The disease is not cosmetic. Periodontal disease is an infection of the tissues that hold the tooth in place, and the bacteria do not stay in the mouth. The AVMA and the American Veterinary Dental College both link advanced dental disease to heart, kidney, and liver problems: the bacteria enter the bloodstream through inflamed gums and seed infection in distant organs. A dental cleaning is not a spa treatment for a pet. It is the treatment of a chronic infection that happens to live in the mouth.

What the bill is made of

A routine dental cleaning for a dog averages $388 nationally, according to CareCredit's 2026 data, with most cleanings falling between $307 and $702. A routine cleaning — scaling, polishing, no extractions — typically runs $350 to $500. That is the floor. The bill climbs from there in steps, and each step is priced separately. Anesthesia runs $100 to $400, and it is not optional: a proper cleaning requires the pet to be still, and the cleaning that matters happens below the gum line, where no awake animal will tolerate an instrument. Pre-anesthetic bloodwork, dental x-rays, and IV fluids are usually additional, and each adds a line to the estimate.

Extractions are where the estimate becomes a range. A simple extraction is a few hundred dollars. A complex extraction — a fractured canine tooth, a molar with multiple roots, a tooth that breaks during removal — runs $500 to $2,500 per tooth. A specialist dental procedure, performed by a board-certified veterinary dentist rather than a general practitioner, starts at $1,500 and climbs from there. A mouth with four complex extractions can cost more than the dog's annual food budget, and the owner learns the number only after the x-rays are taken — which is to say, after the anesthesia has already started.

Why nobody budgets for it

The reason dental disease is the bill nobody budgets for is that it is invisible until it is expensive. A routine visit costs $200 in 2025, according to the AVMA, up from $147 in 2024. An emergency visit averages $808 at the median, according to VetReceipt's analysis of more than 2,900 real invoices. Those are numbers owners can imagine. A dental bill that starts at $388 and ends at $3,000 is a number that arrives as a surprise, because the disease that causes it was invisible for years.

The insurance gap makes the surprise worse. Only 4.5 percent of American pets are insured, according to the North American Pet Health Insurance Association, and most policies that do cover dental work cover it only when the disease is already advanced — not the preventive cleanings that would have stopped it. In a 2025 national survey, only about 66 percent of owners said they could cover a $1,000 lifesaving treatment for their pet. A $2,000 dental bill is not lifesaving in the emergency sense, which makes it harder to justify and harder to finance — and easier to postpone, which is exactly what makes it more expensive next year.

The counterargument: the cheap alternative is not a bargain

There is a cheaper option, and it is worth naming honestly: anesthesia-free dental cleaning, offered by some groomers and non-veterinary providers for a fraction of the price. The American Veterinary Dental College's position is unambiguous — anesthesia-free cleaning removes only the visible tartar above the gum line, where the disease does not live, and it cannot include the x-rays, the probing, or the subgingival scaling that actually treat periodontal disease. A pet that has had an anesthesia-free cleaning looks better and is not better. The tartar is gone. The infection is not.

The anesthesia itself is the part owners fear most, and the fear is understandable — but the risk is small and the alternative is worse. Pre-anesthetic bloodwork, modern monitoring, and IV fluid support have made anesthesia in healthy pets routine. The AVMA's position is that the risk of untreated dental disease, measured in years of chronic infection and organ damage, exceeds the risk of a monitored anesthetic procedure by a wide margin. The bill is real. So is the disease it treats.

What to do before the estimate arrives

First, ask for a dental assessment at every annual visit, starting at age one — not when the dog is ten and the disease is advanced. The cleaning that costs $388 at age three costs $2,500 at age ten, because the disease has had seven years to work. Second, brush the teeth. Daily brushing is the single most effective preventive measure in veterinary dentistry, and it costs less than the toothpaste. Dental chews and water additives help at the margins. Nothing replaces the brush.

Third, budget for the cleaning the way you budget for the annual visit. A $388 cleaning every year is cheaper than a $2,500 extraction once, and it is cheaper than the cardiac and kidney disease that untreated periodontal infection promotes. The bill nobody budgets for is the bill that arrives after seven years of not budgeting. Start the budget the year the dog arrives, and the teeth will never be a surprise.

The prevention math is the part owners rarely do. A dental chew costs a dollar a day. A water additive costs a few dollars a month. A toothbrush and veterinary toothpaste cost less than a single restaurant meal. Against a $388 annual cleaning, those are trivial sums — and against a $2,500 extraction, they are invisible. The AVMA's position is that dental care is not an optional add-on to pet ownership but a routine part of it, like vaccination and parasite control. The owners who spend the dollar a day are not the ones who get the $2,500 estimate. The owners who skip it are — and the estimate arrives seven years later, when the disease has had time to do its work.
