The Specialist's Invoice: What a Referral Vet Visit Actually Costs

The referral letter was two paragraphs long, and the second paragraph was the one that mattered. The regular vet had done what she could. The next step was a cardiologist, forty minutes away, with an estimate that started before anyone knew what was wrong.

5 min read · Prime Domain Capital

The referral letter was two paragraphs long, and the second paragraph was the one that mattered. The regular vet had done what she could: the bloodwork, the x-rays, the first round of medication. The dog was not getting better, and the next step was a cardiologist — a specialist, at a referral hospital, forty minutes away, with an echocardiogram machine that cost more than the regular clinic's entire building. The estimate came with the appointment confirmation: consultation $200 to $500, echocardiogram $400 to $700, and that was before anyone knew what was wrong. The owner did the only math available to her. The dog was eight years old. The regular vet's bill had already reached $1,200. The specialist's invoice had not started yet.

What the specialist's invoice is made of

A referral visit is not a more expensive version of a regular visit. It is a different product. The consultation alone runs $200 to $500, against roughly $200 for a routine visit at a general practice in 2025, according to the American Veterinary Medical Association. The diagnostics are where the numbers climb. An echocardiogram — the ultrasound of the heart that is the cardiologist's core tool — runs $400 to $700. A CT scan runs $1,000 to $2,500. An MRI runs $1,500 to $3,000. These are not prices the specialist invents. They are the amortized cost of machines that cost hundreds of thousands of dollars, operated by technicians the general practice does not employ, interpreted by a veterinarian who spent three additional years in residency after veterinary school.

The surgical numbers are steeper still. A TPLO surgery for a torn cruciate ligament — one of the most common referral procedures — runs $3,000 to $7,000. Hip surgery falls in the same range. Chemotherapy runs $200 to $500 per session, and a course of treatment is measured in sessions, not in single visits. Radiation therapy runs $5,000 to $10,000 for a full course. A single night in a referral hospital's intensive care unit averages $619 for a dog and $582 for a cat at national average prices. The referral hospital is not charging for the same care at a higher price. It is charging for care the general practice cannot provide at any price.

Why the gap keeps widening

The referral premium sits on top of a market that is already rising fast. Americans spent $35.9 billion on veterinary care in 2024, according to the American Pet Products Association, up from $31.4 billion in 2022 and $19.2 billion in 2015. Vet costs have risen roughly 7 percent a year since 2020, outpacing general inflation, and the AVMA puts the average routine visit at $200 in 2025, up from $147 in 2024 — a 36 percent jump in a single year. Emergency care sits at the sharp end of the same curve: the average emergency exam is $135 for a dog and $143 for a cat, before any test or treatment, according to a national CareCredit study of all 50 states.

The structure of the market pushes owners toward the specialist. Corporate ownership of veterinary practices rose from 8 percent to 50 percent since 2014, as this site reported in its earlier coverage of private equity in veterinary care, and the corporate groups have invested heavily in referral hospitals — the high-acuity, high-margin end of the business. A general practice that refers a case out loses the revenue. A corporate group that owns both the general practice and the referral hospital keeps it. The referral is good medicine and good business at the same time, which is exactly why owners should ask hard questions about it.

The counterargument: the specialist earns the premium

None of this means the referral is a bad deal. A board-certified cardiologist who reads a thousand echocardiograms a year will see things a general practitioner will miss — a valve defect at an early stage, a congenital condition that changes the prognosis entirely, a medication interaction that would have been invisible without the imaging. The alternative to the specialist is not cheaper care. It is a wrong diagnosis at a general practice, followed by months of the wrong treatment, followed by the specialist anyway — with the bill for the wrong treatment added to the bill for the right one.

Not every referral is equal, and the credentials are the first thing to check. A board-certified specialist — certified by the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine for cardiology, oncology, and internal medicine, or the American College of Veterinary Surgeons for surgery — has completed a three-year residency and passed a certifying examination after veterinary school. A general practitioner with an interest in cardiology has not. The price difference between the two is the price of that training, and it is worth confirming before the appointment: the referral letter should name the specialist and their credentials, and the hospital's website should list them. A referral to a veterinarian who is not board-certified is not a referral. It is a more expensive general practice visit.

Second, ask whether the general practice can do any of the work. A basic ultrasound, a blood panel, a radiograph — the diagnostics that do not require a specialist's interpretation — can often be done at the regular clinic for a fraction of the referral price, and the specialist can review the results rather than repeat them. The referral hospital will sometimes insist on its own imaging, and sometimes it is right to: an echocardiogram performed by a cardiologist answers different questions than one performed by a general practitioner. But the owner who asks the question saves the cost of the duplicated test when the answer is no. And if the pet is not yet insured, the referral is the moment the question becomes urgent — a condition diagnosed before the policy starts is a pre-existing condition, and no policy covers it.

The equipment argument is real, too. An MRI machine that costs $1 million and runs ten scans a week has to charge $1,500 a scan to pay for itself. The general practice that does not own the machine does not bear the cost, and does not provide the service. The specialist's invoice is the price of capability, and capability is what the owner is buying.

What to do before the referral

First, ask for the written estimate before the appointment, and ask what it does not include. A consultation and an echocardiogram are the floor, not the ceiling: bloodwork, medication, follow-up imaging, and a second visit are usually extra, and the estimate should say so. Second, ask whether the work can be done in stages. Some diagnostics can wait a week. Some cannot. Knowing which is which is worth hundreds of dollars, and the specialist's office will tell you if you ask.

Third, decide in advance how you will pay. Only 4.5 percent of American pets are insured, according to the North American Pet Health Insurance Association, and the average annual premium runs about $640 for dogs and $387 for cats — which means most owners facing a referral bill are paying out of pocket. In a 2025 national survey, only about 66 percent of owners said they could cover a $1,000 lifesaving treatment for their pet. A referral invoice that starts at $700 and ends at $5,000 is the bill that separates the owners who planned from the ones who did not. The planning takes an afternoon. The bill takes a year to pay off.
